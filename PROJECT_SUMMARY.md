# Project summary: Declared

## Overview

Your phone lets apps be given powerful abilities: reading what is on the screen, reading your notifications, installing other apps, acting as an administrator of the phone, and so on. Most people have no easy way to see which apps hold these abilities, where those apps came from, or when something changed. Many security apps answer with a score or a green tick, which tells you nothing you can check.

Declared is an Android app that shows you these things plainly. It lists which apps hold each powerful ability, where each came from (the phone's maker, a store, or installed by hand), and what the ability would let the app do. Every item says what the app saw, how sure it is, and what it cannot tell. It never gives a score and never says your phone is safe.

The first time you open it, it asks you to look through the list and say which apps you recognize. From then on it shows you what has changed since you last looked, for instance an app that is now signed by someone else. While the app is open it notices changes as they happen. It also has a screen about its own limits: what it cannot see on your phone and why, so a quiet screen is never mistaken for a clean phone.

Beyond a list, it joins the facts up. An app that can read the screen is one thing; the same app on a phone whose accounts send sign-in codes by text message is a way into your bank. The app shows these ways in plainly (it calls them attack paths), says how sure it is of each link and what it cannot see on that path, and works out the smallest set of changes that would stop them, with what each change costs. You can try a change in a preview that touches nothing on the phone. Where it cannot tell, it says "not ruled out" instead of staying quiet.

Declared also keeps a history. Each time you open it, it adds a short entry saying what was different from last time, and the entries are tied together and signed with a key that never leaves the phone, so changing an old entry, or removing one from the middle, shows. It can tell you that a fingerprint was added or the screen lock was removed since it last looked, and it shows the stretches when it was not looking as gaps, because it only looks while it is open. It also tells you what it cannot prove: it cannot see the newest entries being cut off, and it does not say your phone was in good order.

It speaks plainly. Jargon is explained in a line where it appears, long text is broken into short lines, and a simple view (bigger text, three places instead of five, one card per thing with one big button to act) is there for anyone new to this. A short tour and a short animated intro explain the rest, including why it is called Declared: every app declares what it can do, and nobody reads that part.

If you want, it can also check your phone on a schedule you choose, anywhere from every 15 minutes to once a year, and send one notification when something needs a look. That is off until you turn it on, and the notification says how many things, never which app.

Everything stays on your phone. The app has no internet permission, no account and no ads. It is open source.

## Complexity Level

Calibrated against the checklist in `project-complexity-and-session-lessons.txt` (Part A), with the evidence for each point.

- **Real depth, not padding.** An attack-path engine: an AND/OR graph over Kleene's three-valued logic (a path that rests on an unreadable fact is "not ruled out", not absent), a weakest-link confidence rule, and the smallest set of fixes as a hitting-set problem (NP-hard) solved exactly by a bounded search tree with a lower bound and deepened one fix at a time, falling back to a greedy answer that is labelled approximate when it reaches its budget. An offline X.509 attestation chain verifier written on `java.security` and a hand-written DER reader (no library), with Google's legacy-root rule, revocation lists and a challenge check; a record diff with the property `apply(old, diff(old, new)) == new` and its inverse; a trust-tier classifier over app origin; a strict JSON parser; an authenticated-encryption file container. Each is there because the product needs it.
- **Core separated from the interface.** `core` is pure Kotlin with no Android import (all of its logic is tested on the JVM), `sensors-android` holds thin adapters behind interfaces, and the Compose app is a view of a pure reducer `(state, event) -> state`.
- **Scale actually pushed.** Report building is tested on 4,000 and 20,000 synthetic apps, with the guard proven by injecting a quadratic scan and watching the test fail; the saved state is round-tripped at 5,000 apps.
- **Layered tests.** 401 JVM tests (unit, property, an exhaustive-subset oracle against the solver on thousands of random graphs, hard hitting-set instances up to 1,800 nodes with measured times, a 3,000-sequence by 80-event random test of the reducer, mutation-proofed guards) and eight emulator conformance scripts (m2 to m9) that drive the real app on an emulator and compare what it says with independent sources (`aapt2`, `apksigner`, `dumpsys`, `settings`, `appops`, the JDK's own certificate verifier, OpenSSL, a separate Python DER reader, and for Stage 2 a second implementation of the attack-path rules in Python with its own brute-force search), plus an instrumented accessibility pass with Google's Accessibility Test Framework at normal and double text size.
- **Documented problem-solving.** A defect log of more than 50 entries, including defects found only by using the running app or by a conformance script (device-admin policies unreadable without one manifest flag; a proxy that stayed in effect after its setting was deleted; a reducer bug found by random sequences; a service that vanished from the report when a test tool suspended it; a solver test that could not fail). The log, the design record and the measured findings are in the repository.
- **Tamper-evident history with an independent verifier.** A hash-linked, ECDSA-signed ledger of snapshot differences under a Keystore key (canonical text written by hand so another program can rebuild it), Keystore tripwires for a fingerprint added and a screen lock removed, and a check tied to the key the phone holds. A second verifier in another language (`hashlib` and the OpenSSL command line, no shared code) accepts a history the app wrote, rejects every alteration, and passes a scan of every character of a sample; a mutation scan of the app's own check found a real hole (stripped signatures), and thinking through who the history must stop found another (a history rewritten under a new key). All of it is checked on the emulator with real fingerprint enrollment and lock changes, 59 checks.
- **Grounded in real standards and data.** Android's own attestation specification and Google's published roots and status list, Android's package-visibility rules (measured recall per version), and a measured live-change experiment (110 changes in five conditions).
- **Extensibility by design.** `core` accepts observations from any sensor, so a desktop or second-device sensor can be added without touching it; this is checkable by reading the dependencies (none on Android).
- **Optional background work, done carefully.** Automatic checks use one repeating system job (no service that stays running, no new library), read the phone with the same code as the screen, compare with the saved baseline through the same pure reducer, and announce a change at most once (what was announced is kept as short hashes, so no app name is written to disk). The notification says how many and never which app, and shows only the app name on a locked screen. The emulator script reads the scheduled job's interval back from `dumpsys jobscheduler` for the default, a preset and a typed value, fires the job by hand, counts the notifications, and checks that turning it off removes the job.
- **The interface treated as engineering.** Findings of one kind are grouped by a tested pure function; the place in every tab survives opening a finding; jargon is found and explained by a tested glossary; one theme is scaled a fifth for the simple view; the accessibility pass covers every screen, the tour, the intro and the simple view at normal and double text size; a debug-only screenshot switch is absent from the release build because it lives in a separate source set.
- **Release engineering.** A signed bundle built with a key kept outside the repository and supplied through the environment, the merged manifest checked for exactly five permissions plus AndroidX's own, native libraries checked for 16 KB alignment, and a logo kit with a template for every future app of the same maker.
- **Build gates.** The build itself refuses a manifest that gains the internet permission (or any permission beyond the five it names), refuses a release with a placeholder application ID, and treats compiler and lint warnings as errors.

Honest scope limits: the full set of scripts is verified on emulators (Android 10 and 12 to 17 for the earlier stages, Android 14 for the newest screens and the automatic checks), not yet on a physical phone with secure hardware; the trademark search and the Play submission are still open.

## Future Scope / Upgrade Opportunities

- **Run the newest scripts across the versions and on a real phone.** The earlier scripts were run on seven Android versions; the new screens and the automatic checks have been run on Android 14. Repeat them on the other images and on a physical phone, then record where results differ (job timing in particular varies by phone maker).
- **More rules and a user study for the paths.** The two threat-model paths that need Stage 4's sensors (a malicious app scanning the local network) or a declared-only fact (a SIM swap), rules read from signed data instead of code, and a few people using the paths screen to see whether it changes what they do.
- **Let automatic checks write to the history.** They read and tell, and write nothing, so the timeline does not show them. Recording them would fill the gaps, at the cost of two writers to one signed file. Also: exporting the latest entry's fingerprint, and running the tripwires on a phone with secure hardware and a real fingerprint sensor.
- **Stage 4, network integrity checks.** DNSSEC, Certificate Transparency and hash-pinned content checks, the first stage with an internet permission and therefore a new privacy page, listing and gate. First to cut if time runs short.
- **Hardware attestation on real phones** through the product's own verifier (StrongBox, other root types, the legacy-root path on a real factory chain), plus a refresh of the bundled reference data that the user opts into.
- **String resources and translation.** Texts live in `core` and are English only; moving them to resources is the prerequisite for translation and for a reading-level review.
- **A user study.** A few people running the first review and rating whether the wording made them understand an app they would otherwise have ignored.
- **Release.** The signed bundle, the listing, the graphics and the form answers are ready (`STORE_LISTING.md`). Left, and only the maker can do them: the Play account, the trademark and name check, a public privacy-policy address, a closed test with enough testers, then production.
- **A Bengali version.** Texts live in `core`, so translating means moving them to resources first; a Bengali listing follows the translated app.
- **More apps under one mark.** The logo kit in `brand/` is the template: the H stays, each app adds one small thing and one accent color.
